## 🔥 Hot list: Agent Context picks

### [Tools Need Not Speak in Text: Latent Context Compression for Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06500)
- Heat: HF 👍6 (4 days on the list, +6); code [THU-Agent/Coca-cola](https://github.com/THU-Agent/Coca-cola) ★7; no X data.
- Problem: Agents append raw tool output to the context. After many steps it is long and noisy, which dilutes attention, and text-level pruning or summarization compresses only so far.
- Method: Coca-Cola compresses tool observations into continuous latent tokens placed in the agent's input embedding space. Action-Preserving Training aligns the next-action distribution before and after compression, so the latent tokens keep the information the decision depends on.
- Result: On 4 QA benchmarks and web-search agents from two model families, token use drops 70.01% to 89.24% and end-to-end time drops 24.45% to 55.97%, while task performance keeps improving.
- Our take: The most direct context-compression paper this edition, so it goes first.

### [Adversarial Speculative Reasoning Improves Long Horizon Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05345)
- Heat: HF 👍10 (5 days on the list, +6); no code.
- Problem: Speculative decoding is usually treated as a pure speedup. The authors find that numerical noise in the verification window accumulates and, after about 30 steps, shifts the output distribution of long-horizon agents and hurts reasoning accuracy.
- Method: They identify critical reasoning points in agent trajectories and train the draft model to be "deliberately rejected" at those points, so the target model samples the critical thinking tokens under minimal noise. Only 0.06% to 0.28% of hosted parameters are trained, and the target model and its output distribution are unchanged.
- Result: End-to-end success improves on complex agentic coding benchmarks. The abstract reframes speculative decoding as an "allocator of context precision."
- Our take: A new angle, tying speculative decoding to long-horizon context precision. The abstract gives no specific improvement figure.

### [SwarmEvolve: Latent Agent Swarms for Evolutionary Discovery and Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08483)
- Heat: HF 👍10 (3 days on the list, +10); code [Qile-J/SwarmEvolve](https://github.com/Qile-J/SwarmEvolve) ★14.
- Problem: LLM-guided evolutionary search generates a long chain of thought every round and keeps regenerating to preserve population diversity, which is slow.
- Method: Each round, hundreds of cheap agents work as a swarm. They reason in latent space without decoding to text and share information by passing KV cache. A novelty filter keeps the most distinct ideas by distance between hidden-state vectors, and a summarizer agent receives the merged KV cache of all members and crosses ideas in latent space.
- Result: It has the highest average score across 21 math-discovery and scientific-computing problems. It reaches in a median of 3 to 8 minutes the best score other SOTA frameworks reach in 60 minutes. With Qwen3 8B it improves on solutions AlphaEvolve found with frontier models on several problems.

### [The SIMPLE Law for Energy Cost of LLM Inference](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03330)
- Heat: HF 👍10 (6 days on the list, +10); no code.
- Problem: No analytical model explains the real energy cost per token. The current SOTA is an empirical model that must be fitted per model.
- Method: The authors derive closed-form compute and memory-access complexity for MHA and GQA, giving a four-parameter SIMPLE model. It separates prefill from decode and splits decode into KV cache cost and next-token compute.
- Result: On 13 dense LLMs on H100, R²=0.969 and MAPE 11.97%, 3.1 to 3.5 times lower than SOTA, with no per-model calibration. Agentic serving uses 13.0 times the energy per request of non-agentic serving. Prefix caching removes about 93% of prefill cost, but the KV cache term is unaffected, so a cached agent request still costs 4.8 times more.
- Our take: Directly relevant to prompt-cache economics. The takeaway is that caching cannot remove the KV term.

### [GEPA-Curator: Curated Learning for Self Improving Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37651)
- Heat: HF 👍8 (6 days on the list, +3); no code.
- Problem: Reflective prompt optimizers such as GEPA learn from failed trajectories, but a failure may come from a weak prompt, a harness limit, randomness or a noisy judge, so it is not always a useful signal.
- Method: Task curation filters out noisy, unreliable tasks. Retrieval of earlier successful trajectories selectively pulls past successes for a task and gives them to the prompt optimizer as extra context.
- Result: On GoBrowse, ALFWorld, AppWorld and τ-bench, with Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 5 as optimizers, it matches or beats the mean of two baselines. It is up to 20.7% above GEPA and 21.2% above ACE.

### [Can Large Language Model Agents Learn by Watching Tutorial Videos?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01460)
- Heat: HF 👍5 (6 days on the list, +3); no code.
- Problem: LLM agents are frozen after training and cannot use the huge pool of tutorial videos, and updating parameters is too costly.
- Method: Tutorial Agentic Learning (TAL) runs tutorial videos through a two-stage adversarial process (mine video-related hard problems, then use them to refine memory) and consolidates them into a persistent, structured "tutorialbook" memory. At inference it retrieves through a table-of-contents tree, tying retrieval to model reasoning instead of embedding similarity.
- Result: On VidLearn, built from 72 chess tutorial videos (12 openings), it beats all baselines and improves monotonically as videos are added. Relative to the raw MLLM it gains up to 113% on the train split and 91% on the test split. In live play, 69% of games have a better opening position than the vanilla model.
- Our take: The test domain is chess plus a second domain the abstract doesn't name, so the evidence for generalization is thin.

### [RoboArchitect: Automating the Design of Robotic Agents with Persistent Memory](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42141)
- Heat: HF 👍8 (7 days on the list, +3); code [roboarchitect-ai/RoboArchitect](https://github.com/roboarchitect-ai/RoboArchitect) ★10.
- Problem: A frozen robot policy performs much better inside a harness, but harnesses are hand-designed for each robot and benchmark.
- Method: A coding model watches robot rollouts, diagnoses failures and iteratively edits the harness around the frozen policy, with no model weight updates. The harness also grows its own persistent memory, carrying learned experience (such as effective grasp depth) into later decisions.
- Result: Average success on unseen scenes rises from 9.7% to 43.3% (4.5 times), and on unseen tasks from 12.7% to 40.2% (3.2 times).

## Observations

- Context compression and caching are the main thread. On the hot list, [Coca-Cola](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06500) (latent-token compression of tool outputs), [SwarmEvolve](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08483) (KV cache passing) and [SIMPLE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03330) (energy cost of KV cache and prefix caching) all target long-context cost. In New, [Nebula](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09474) and [DARIC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09109) do the same through KV reuse.
- Harness self-evolution is shifting from "can it work" to "can it be trusted." The hot-list [continual-evolution forgetting study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03219) (SWE-Bench 94.0% to 32.0%) and New's [Beneath the Surface](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07624) (all 16 evolved agents gain and lose) point to the same problem: a higher benchmark score does not mean earlier coverage is kept. Automatic harness design such as [RoboArchitect](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.42141) and TinyAstral continues to advance.
- Memory research covers both trade-offs and safety. New has the [compaction vs RAG comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09807) and the [MemoryDojo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09721) poisoning benchmark. On the hot list, [TAL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01460) and [GEPA-Curator](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.37651) both use retrieval of past experience as a way to learn without updating parameters.
